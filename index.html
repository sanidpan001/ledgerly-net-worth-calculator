    Net Worth, Explained: The One Number That Summarises Your Financial Life

    Ask most people how they are doing financially and they will quote their salary. Ask a banker, a financial planner or a business owner the same question and they will talk about net worth. The difference matters. Income is a flow — money passing through your hands each month. Net worth is a stock — what remains after the flow has done its work. It is the single most honest summary of your financial position, because it counts everything you own and everything you owe in one calculation, and it is exactly what Ledgerly helps you measure.

    The formula could not be simpler: net worth equals total assets minus total liabilities. An asset is anything you own that holds measurable value — the balance in your bank accounts, money in retirement and brokerage accounts, the market value of your home, vehicles, business equity, and possessions you could realistically sell. A liability is anything you owe — a mortgage, a car loan, student debt, personal loans, and credit card balances. Subtract the second list from the first and the result, positive or negative, is your net worth.

    Why a Balance Sheet Beats a Budget for the Big Picture

    Budgets are essential for managing the month, but they cannot tell you whether you are actually getting ahead. You can feel comfortable on a high income while quietly falling behind, if every raise is matched by a larger car payment and a growing card balance. The balance sheet exposes that instantly. When you lay assets and liabilities side by side — the two-column view Ledgerly is built around — patterns jump out: a home whose equity is growing nicely, an investment account that has been neglected, a cluster of small debts that together outweigh your savings. Wealth is built in that gap between the two columns, and you cannot manage a gap you never look at.

    There is also a psychological benefit. Income comparisons are endless and usually discouraging, because someone always earns more. Net worth comparisons are personal: the most meaningful benchmark is your own number from six or twelve months ago. Watching the figure climb — even slowly — turns abstract advice about saving and debt into visible progress, and visible progress is the strongest motivator personal finance has to offer.

    What Counts, and the Judgement Calls

    Most entries are straightforward. Cash and deposits count at face value. Investments count at current market value, not what you paid for them. Property counts at a realistic selling price, ideally minus the costs of selling. Vehicles count at resale value, which is usually far less than the purchase price — a humbling but healthy correction. On the other side, count the full outstanding balance of every debt, including the mortgage, and do not forget informal obligations if you genuinely intend to repay them.

    A few items spark debate. Should you include the car you need for work, or your furniture and electronics? A practical rule: include anything you could sell for a meaningful amount, and value it conservatively. Should you include future pension or social security income? Most personal balance sheets leave it out, because it is an income stream rather than an owned asset, though some planners add a conservative estimate. Whatever conventions you choose, consistency matters more than perfection — use the same rules each time so the trend is meaningful.

    Benchmarks: What Is a Good Net Worth for Your Age?

    Because net worth grows with time and earnings, raw comparisons between a 28-year-old and a 58-year-old are meaningless. Benchmarks solve this by scaling expectations to age and income. The best known comes from the book The Millionaire Next Door: expected net worth equals your age multiplied by your annual pre-tax income, divided by ten. A 40-year-old earning 90,000 a year would have a benchmark of 360,000. The authors called people at twice that level prodigious accumulators and those below half of it under-accumulators. It is a demanding yardstick — it assumes years of saving well above average — so treat it as a compass, not a verdict.

    Simpler milestone frameworks exist too. One common version suggests holding roughly one times your annual income by your mid-thirties, three times by your late forties, and six to eight times by retirement. Early in your career a negative or near-zero net worth is entirely normal: student loans arrive years before serious saving can begin, and the typical trajectory is a slow climb through the twenties, acceleration in the thirties and forties as debts fall and compounding takes over, and a peak near retirement.     The Levers That Move Your Net Worth

    Only four things can change your net worth: earning more, spending less than you earn, earning returns on what you own, and reducing what you owe. Every financial strategy is a combination of these levers. Paying down a credit card at twenty percent interest is arithmetically identical to earning a guaranteed twenty percent return — which is why high-interest debt repayment is almost always the first move. Increasing contributions to tax-advantaged retirement accounts pulls two levers at once: it converts income into assets and shelters the growth. Growing home equity works quietly in the background, as each mortgage payment shifts a little value from the liability column to the asset column.

    The debt-to-assets ratio, shown alongside your net worth in Ledgerly, captures your leverage in one figure. Below twenty percent is very comfortable; above sixty percent means creditors effectively have a large claim on everything you own, and income shocks become dangerous. Tracking the ratio alongside the headline number prevents a subtle trap: net worth can rise while risk rises too, if both columns grow together through borrowing.

    How Often Should You Measure?

    Quarterly is the sweet spot for most households, with monthly checks if you are actively paying down debt and want the encouragement. More frequent measurement mostly adds noise, because investment values fluctuate daily without changing your underlying position. The snapshot feature in Ledgerly exists precisely for this rhythm: save a dated snapshot each time you do a full review, and the trend sparkline converts a pile of numbers into a story. A rising line through a flat market tells you your habits are working; a flat line despite a rising income tells you lifestyle creep is eating the difference.

    Finally, remember what net worth does not measure. It says nothing about liquidity — a household can be asset-rich and cash-poor, with wealth locked in a home while an emergency looms. It says nothing about insurance, job security or health. Treat it as the headline indicator of a dashboard, not the whole dashboard. But as headlines go, it is the best one personal finance has: honest, complete, and entirely within your power to improve, one column at a time.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    How do I calculate my net worth?

    Add up everything you own that has value — cash, investments, property, vehicles — then subtract everything you owe, such as mortgages, loans and credit card balances. The result is your net worth.

    What counts as an asset in a net worth calculation?

    Assets are things you own with measurable value: bank balances, retirement and brokerage accounts, real estate, vehicles, business equity and valuable possessions you could realistically sell.

    What counts as a liability?

    Liabilities are debts you owe: mortgages, car loans, student loans, personal loans, credit card balances and any other amount you are obligated to repay.

    What is a good net worth by age?

    A classic benchmark from The Millionaire Next Door expects net worth of roughly your age multiplied by your annual income, divided by ten. Reaching one times your income by your mid-30s and three times by your late 40s are other common milestones.

    How often should I recalculate my net worth?

    Most people benefit from checking quarterly or monthly. Frequent enough to notice trends and celebrate progress, but not so often that daily market swings cause stress.
